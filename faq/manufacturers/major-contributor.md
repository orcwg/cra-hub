# If a company is a major contributor to an open source project but doesn't monetise it, is it the manufacturer?

No. Contributing to an open source project, however much, does not make a company its manufacturer. What makes a manufacturer is selling the software: publishing it under your own name or trademark and supplying it in the course of a commercial activity ([[CRA Article 3(13)]], [[CRA Article 3(22)]]).

The CRA _"does not apply to natural or legal persons who contribute with source code"_ to open source software _"that are not under their responsibility"_, and _"the mere fact that an open-source software product with digital elements receives financial support from manufacturers or that manufacturers contribute to the development of such a product should not in itself determine that the activity is of commercial nature"_ ([[CRA Recital 18]]). The Commission's guidance draws the line between contributors and the people responsible for a project: a project is under the responsibility of those who _"publish it and exercise primary control over its development, releases, and distribution decisions"_; everyone else who contributes code is a contributor, whatever the size of their contribution, and commit access alone changes nothing ([[CRA-GUIDANCE point 49]], [[CRA-GUIDANCE Example 13]]). A company whose developers contribute to a project someone else controls has no obligations for that project under the CRA, even when it is the manufacturer of other products, and even when it builds the project into them ([[CRA-GUIDANCE point 86]], [[CRA-GUIDANCE Example 30]], [[CRA-GUIDANCE Example 31]], [[CRA-GUIDANCE Example 32]]). For a company supporting a project it does not control, see [[manufacturers/contributors]] and [[manufacturers/funding-foss-dev]].

If the company does more than contribute, and is the one publishing the project and controlling its releases, the project is under its responsibility. It is still not the manufacturer as long as it does not monetise the project: open source software _"not monetised by their manufacturers should not be considered to be a commercial activity"_ ([[CRA Recital 18]], [[CRA-GUIDANCE point 42]]). Being a legal person, the company is then the project's open-source software steward if it supports the project on a sustained basis and the project is intended for commercial use, with the lighter obligations of [[CRA Article 24]] ([[CRA Article 3(14)]], [[CRA-GUIDANCE point 74]], [[CRA-GUIDANCE point 76]], [[CRA-GUIDANCE Example 29]]). Only the publisher can be the steward: in the guidance's example, the company publishing and maintaining a project is its steward, while the company contributing code and developers' time to it _"has no obligations under the CRA for that FOSS"_ ([[CRA-GUIDANCE Example 31]]). See [[stewards/what-is-a-steward]] and [[stewards/for-profit]].

"Doesn't monetise it" has to hold in full. A company that gives the software away but uses it as the platform through which it sells other products or services, or makes processing users' personal data a condition of use, is monetising it and is its manufacturer ([[CRA Recital 15]], [[CRA-GUIDANCE point 54]]). See [[cra-itself/monetisation]].

For users of the project, this means the contributing company is not a manufacturer they can turn to. A company's manufacturer obligations attach to the products it sells, project by project, not to everything it works on ([[CRA-GUIDANCE point 72]], [[CRA-GUIDANCE point 73]]).
