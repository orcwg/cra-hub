# If a company is a major contributor to an open source project but doesn't monetise it, is it the manufacturer?

No. Contributing to an open source project, however much, does not make a company its manufacturer. The Commission's guidance says so in terms: _"A manufacturer that has contributed to the development of certain features does not become the manufacturer of that FOSS component"_ ([[CRA-GUIDANCE Example 32]]), and companies that build a project into their own products _"do not become responsible for such components' individual compliance with the CRA, even where the manufacturers contribute source code to their maintenance"_ ([[CRA-GUIDANCE point 86]]). The CRA itself says that how a project is developed or financed does not decide whether it is a commercial activity ([[CRA Recital 18]], [[CRA-GUIDANCE point 42]]).

What makes a manufacturer is selling the software. The guidance defines one as the person or organisation that _"supplies a product with digital elements under its name or trademark, and that does so in the course of a commercial activity"_ ([[CRA-GUIDANCE point 47]], [[CRA Article 3(13)]]), and draws the line between those responsible for a project and everyone else: a project is under the responsibility of those who _"publish it and exercise primary control over its development, releases, and distribution decisions"_; everyone else who contributes code is a contributor, whatever the size of their contribution, and commit access alone changes nothing ([[CRA-GUIDANCE point 49]], [[CRA-GUIDANCE Example 13]]). In the guidance's examples, a company contributing code and developers' time to a project that another company or a foundation publishes has _"no obligations under the CRA for that FOSS"_, even when it builds the project into products it sells ([[CRA-GUIDANCE Example 30]], [[CRA-GUIDANCE Example 31]], [[CRA-GUIDANCE Example 32]]). For a company supporting a project it does not control, see [[manufacturers/contributors]] and [[manufacturers/funding-foss-dev]].

If the company does more than contribute, and is the one publishing the project and controlling its releases, the guidance's answer is that it is the project's steward, not its manufacturer, as long as it does not monetise the project. In its example, a company that publishes and maintains a project under its own name but _"does not charge for its use or monetise in other ways"_ is _"not its manufacturer, but is its steward"_ ([[CRA-GUIDANCE Example 29]], [[CRA-GUIDANCE point 76]]). That holds where the company supports the project on a sustained basis and the project is intended for commercial use, with the lighter obligations of [[CRA Article 24]] ([[CRA Article 3(14)]], [[CRA-GUIDANCE point 74]]). See [[stewards/what-is-a-steward]] and [[stewards/for-profit]].

"Doesn't monetise it" has to hold in full. The guidance says a company that gives the software away but provides it as _"the software through which it monetises other products with digital elements or services"_, or makes processing users' personal data a condition of use, is placing it on the market and is its manufacturer ([[CRA Recital 15]], [[CRA-GUIDANCE point 54]]). See [[cra-itself/monetisation]].

For users of the project, this means the contributing company is not a manufacturer they can turn to. The guidance says a company's role is decided project by project: _"the same legal entity can be required to fulfil different roles for different FOSS projects"_, manufacturer for one and steward for another ([[CRA-GUIDANCE point 72]], [[CRA-GUIDANCE point 73]]).
