# Do all open source projects have a _manufacturer_?

No. Most open source projects have no manufacturer, and the CRA does not require one to be identified or appointed.

A project has a manufacturer only when someone sells it: supplies it in the EU in the course of a commercial activity, under their own name or trademark ([[CRA Article 3(13)]], [[CRA Article 3(22)]]). Open source software that the people publishing it do not monetise is not supplied commercially ([[CRA Recital 18]], [[CRA-GUIDANCE point 42]]), and the Commission's guidance is explicit that _"whether the CRA applies to a given FOSS component depends solely on whether the natural or legal person that publishes it places it on the market"_ ([[CRA-GUIDANCE point 87]]). A project that is not placed on the market _"falls outside the scope of the CRA"_, unless its publisher is a company or foundation that meets the definition of an open-source software steward ([[CRA-GUIDANCE point 43]]). What counts as monetising a project: see [[maintainers/monetization]] and [[cra-itself/monetisation]].

Most open source projects are not monetised by the people who publish them, so most have no manufacturer. Many have no steward either: a steward has to be a company, foundation or other legal person that systematically supports the project on a sustained basis, and most projects have no such organisation behind them (see [[stewards/do-all-projects-have-stewards]]). A project that an individual or a loose group of volunteers publishes without monetising it has neither a manufacturer nor a steward, and no obligations under the CRA ([[CRA Article 3(14)]], [[CRA-GUIDANCE point 53]], [[CRA-GUIDANCE Example 27]], [[CRA-GUIDANCE Example 34]]).

This stays true however widely the project is used in commercial products. The companies that build it into something they sell are the manufacturers of their own products, not of the project, and their doing so _"has no impact on the status of that FOSS component under the CRA"_ ([[CRA-GUIDANCE point 87]]; see [[manufacturers/multiple-manufacturers]]). Nor does financial support or contributed code from such companies give the project a manufacturer ([[CRA Recital 18]]; see [[manufacturers/major-contributor]]).

For users, this means there is nobody to hold to manufacturer obligations for an open source project that nobody sells. The CRA's protection applies at the point where a company sells a product built on it: that company has to exercise due diligence on the open source components it integrates, including those the CRA does not cover, and to handle vulnerabilities in its product as a whole for the whole of its support period ([[CRA Article 13(5)]], [[CRA Article 13(8)]], [[CRA-GUIDANCE point 88]], [[official/faq_4-4-4]]).
