# What level of due diligence is reasonable for open source components?

The level of due diligence reasonable for an open source component is the same as for any other third-party component. The Commission's FAQ says so: _"the appropriate level of due diligence is dependent on the nature and level of cybersecurity risk of a given open-source component"_, the same rule it gives for components in general, and you are allowed to integrate open source components that are outside the scope of the CRA as well as components published by an open-source software steward ([[official/faq_4-4-4]], [[official/faq_4-4-2]]). The CRA sets no fixed list of checks and no stricter rule for open source: what it requires is that the component doesn't compromise the security of your product ([[CRA Article 13(5)]]). Article 13(5) names open source only to make clear that the obligation also covers components that were never sold, and the scenarios in the Commission's guidance all end the same way: whoever publishes the project, the company that integrates it _"needs to exercise due diligence in accordance with Article 13(5)"_ ([[CRA-GUIDANCE point 88]], [[CRA-GUIDANCE Example 34]]).

What due diligence requires, and how Recital 34's actions and the Commission's further examples fit in, is covered in [[due-diligence/recital-34]]. For an open source component, the checks that fit are typically whether the project is maintained and ships security updates (its security update history), whether vulnerabilities in the European vulnerability database or other public databases affect the version you integrate, what the project's security documentation says about the functions you rely on, its SBOM where it has one, how long the project commits to supporting the version you use, and your own tests where the risk warrants them ([[official/faq_4-4-2]], [[CRA-GUIDANCE point 171]]). A component your product depends on for cryptography or updates needs more of this than a formatting library.

Three things are different about open source in practice:

- **There is usually no CE marking to rely on.** The Commission's FAQ says you can integrate components that don't bear the CE marking, including open source components outside the scope of the CRA, as long as they don't compromise the security of your product ([[official/faq_4-4-1]]). The developers of such a component have no obligations under the CRA ([[CRA-GUIDANCE Example 34]]), so the first of Recital 34's actions doesn't apply and you use the others. This does not mean you have to bring the component into compliance with Annex I yourself: see [[due-diligence/essential-requirements]].
- **Projects can make this easier.** A project with a steward has a documented cybersecurity policy covering secure development and vulnerability handling ([[CRA Article 24]]), which gives you something to check. Any project can publish documentation that helps you verify what you rely on: see [[due-diligence/support]]. The Commission's FAQ adds that the voluntary security attestation programmes the CRA lets the Commission set up for open source software _"would facilitate manufacturers' due diligence obligation"_ where available ([[official/faq_4-4-4]], [[CRA Article 25]]); none exists yet, and integrating only attested components is not required: see [[attestations/what-is-a-security-attestation]].
- **Vulnerability handling stays with you.** The Commission's FAQ says your vulnerability handling obligations cover your product in its entirety, including every integrated component, and that where the people behind an open source component don't support you in fixing a vulnerability, you address it by other means: disabling the function, switching components, or patching it yourself ([[official/faq_4-3-6]]). A patch you develop for the component has to be shared with the project, and the vulnerability reported to it ([[CRA Article 13(6)]]).
