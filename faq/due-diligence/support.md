# How can an open source project or its steward support manufacturers' due diligence through documentation?

An open source project, or the steward that supports it, can help the manufacturers who integrate its software meet their due diligence obligation by publishing the information those manufacturers have to check anyway: what the software is for and how it is built, what it contains, how it is kept secure, and how to report a vulnerability to you. Publishing it is voluntary, and it does not move any responsibility from the manufacturer to you.

Neither the Commission's FAQ nor its guidance says what an open source project should publish: both describe due diligence from the manufacturer's side. That is the hub's starting point, because what a manufacturer has to check is what you can document. Manufacturers have to exercise _due diligence_ (take appropriate care) when they integrate a component made by someone else, including open source software that is not sold, so that the component does not compromise the security of their product ([[CRA Article 13(5)]], [[official/faq_4-4-1]]). The Commission's guidance says the evidence they use for that _"may consist of documentation obtained from the component manufacturer, such as technical specifications, security documentation or relevant conformity or assurance documentation"_, with tests where appropriate ([[CRA-GUIDANCE point 171]]). The Commission's FAQ lists the checks a manufacturer may make: whether the component receives regular security updates, whether it has known vulnerabilities in public databases, whether it has been tested, its SBOM, its support period, whether its intended purpose fits the manufacturer's use, and the security practices of the people who make it ([[official/faq_4-4-2]], [[CRA Recital 34]]). Where the documentation that answers these questions is missing, every manufacturer has to work it out alone; where a project publishes it once, every manufacturer can rely on it. What manufacturers are expected to do on their side is covered in [[due-diligence/foss]].

Documentation that answers the Commission's questions is what a manufacturer can use:

- **Design and architecture.** The Commission's guidance gives the example of a product that _"relies on cryptographic functions, update mechanisms or secure communications provided by a component"_: the manufacturer has to verify that the component provides them ([[CRA-GUIDANCE point 171]]), and that your software's intended purpose fits its use ([[official/faq_4-4-2]]). A description of your project's architecture, its security-relevant functions (authentication, cryptography, update mechanism) and its intended use gives manufacturers what they need for that, and for the description of their own product's architecture that their technical documentation has to include ([[CRA Annex VII]]).
- **Software bill of materials (SBOM).** The Commission's FAQ lists _"reviewing the SBOM of that component"_, when one is available, among the due diligence actions ([[official/faq_4-4-2]]). You don't have to produce one ([[stewards/sbom]]), but an SBOM published with your releases lets manufacturers build theirs on it and track vulnerabilities in your dependencies ([[CRA Recital 77]]).
- **Security updates and support.** Which versions receive security fixes, for how long, and where the history of security releases is published, so that manufacturers can check your _"security updates history"_ and the support period of what they integrate ([[official/faq_4-4-2]], [[CRA Recital 34]]).
- **Vulnerability handling.** A security policy saying how to report a vulnerability to you, where you publish security advisories, and how you want security fixes shared with you. Manufacturers handle vulnerabilities in their own products themselves ([[official/faq_4-3-6]]), but this documentation is still used: when they find a vulnerability in your software they have to report it to you and share their fix ([[CRA Article 13(6)]]), and the Commission's guidance tells them to follow your _"security policies, coordinated vulnerability disclosure processes or designated channels for reporting vulnerabilities"_, to check your advisories and issue tracker first to avoid duplicate reports, and to follow your guidelines for sharing fixes ([[CRA-GUIDANCE point 223]], [[CRA-GUIDANCE point 227]]). A steward's cybersecurity policy, which it has to document anyway, covers much of this ([[CRA Article 24(1)]], [[stewards/documentation]]).
- **Test results and security assessments.** Reports of security testing, audits or assessments the project has had, where they exist: _"assurance documentation"_ of this kind is evidence a manufacturer can rely on before deciding whether to run tests of its own ([[CRA-GUIDANCE point 171]], [[official/faq_4-4-2]]).

The CRA does not say who publishes this information. A project can publish it itself, and a steward can publish it on behalf of the projects it supports; nothing in the CRA or the Commission's guidance stops either. The only documentation a steward has to produce is its cybersecurity policy ([[CRA Article 24]]); everything above is optional.

Publishing it does not make you responsible for the manufacturer's compliance. The Commission's guidance is explicit that maintainers of open source components that are not placed on the market _"do not bear obligations in relation to other entities that may integrate such components into their products with digital elements"_ ([[CRA-GUIDANCE point 87]]), and that the manufacturers who integrate them keep their own due diligence obligation ([[CRA-GUIDANCE point 88]]). The manufacturer still has to draw up its own technical documentation ([[CRA Article 31]], [[official/faq_6-6]]); yours is evidence it can use, not a substitute.

Security attestations are the other way the CRA foresees to make this easier. The Commission's FAQ says the Commission _"is empowered to establish voluntary security attestations programmes that can be used to assess the conformity of free and open-source components with the CRA. Where available, such attestation programmes would facilitate manufacturers' due diligence obligation"_ ([[official/faq_4-4-4]], [[CRA Article 25]]). They are to be designed so that not only the project but also third parties, _"such as manufacturers that integrate such products into their own products with digital elements, users, or Union and national public administrations"_, can _"initiate or finance a security attestation"_ ([[CRA Recital 21]]): a way for the manufacturers who benefit from this work to pay for it, rather than each repeating it on their own. No such programme exists yet; see [[attestations/what-is-a-security-attestation]].
