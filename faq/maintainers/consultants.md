# Do common consulting arrangements make me a manufacturer?

No, common consulting arrangements do not make you a manufacturer. Helping a client install, configure or integrate software is providing a service, not placing a product on the market. The CRA applies to products, not to services, so providing a service does not make you a manufacturer, even when the software is open source ([[CRA-GUIDANCE point 59]], [[CRA-GUIDANCE Example 19]], see [[manufacturers/service-providers]]). The exception is a service that substantially modifies the software, which can make the provider its manufacturer ([[CRA-GUIDANCE point 59]], [[CRA Article 22]]).

This also holds for consulting around a project you publish yourself. Selling implementation, integration, training or other professional services for your own open source software does not, by itself, make you its manufacturer, as long as the software can be downloaded, installed and updated freely and your services are optional ([[CRA-GUIDANCE point 55]], [[CRA-GUIDANCE point 56]], [[CRA-GUIDANCE Example 18]]). What would make you a manufacturer is selling the software: a paid edition, or a version whose updates, maintenance or technical assistance only paying customers get. That version is a product you sell, and you have the manufacturer's obligations for it ([[CRA-GUIDANCE point 57]], [[CRA-GUIDANCE Example 17]]).

Those obligations apply only to the version you sell. For a free version of the same project, you have the obligations of an open-source software steward if you are a company or foundation (a legal person, see [[legislation/legal-person]]) that provides sustained support for its development. If you are an individual (a natural person), the free version falls outside the CRA entirely ([[CRA-GUIDANCE point 52]], [[CRA-GUIDANCE point 53]]).
