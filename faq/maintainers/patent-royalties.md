# Do patent royalties on open source software make me a manufacturer?

Patent royalties make you the manufacturer of your open source project when they are the price of using it: when the right to use the software depends on paying them. Royalties earned on the patent from people who do not get them with your software do not. Neither the CRA nor the Commission's guidance mentions patents or royalties; what they say about prices applies.

The guidance's test is whether access to the software itself _"is conditioned on remuneration"_ ([[CRA-GUIDANCE point 56]]). Where you charge _"a price for the software itself"_, you are placing it on the market and are its manufacturer ([[CRA-GUIDANCE point 51]]). A royalty your users have to pay you to run your software is such a price, whatever it is called, and the version they pay for is a product you sell, with a manufacturer's obligations ([[CRA Article 13]]; see [[maintainers/become-manufacturer]]). A licence under which anyone can use the software without paying royalties, as the common open source licences with patent grants provide, keeps it free.

Royalties that do not come with the software are different. A company that licenses your patent for its own, separate implementation pays for the patent, not for your software, and that income no more makes your project commercial than a grant or a salary does: the CRA looks at whether the software is supplied commercially, not at where your money comes from ([[CRA Recital 18]]; see [[maintainers/grants]]).

The cost-recovery rule does not help here. The CRA and the guidance let an individual recover actual costs through donations or paid support without that being a commercial activity ([[CRA Recital 15]], [[CRA-GUIDANCE point 58]]); neither extends that to a price for the software. Which costs count, and whether past development costs can be spread over time, is not settled by either: see [[cra-itself/actual-costs]].
