# Can open source projects control who is allowed to issue security attestations for them?

No, open source projects cannot directly control who is allowed to issue security attestations for them. They do keep two practical safeguards: choosing which attestations they promote, and protecting their trademarks.

Projects can control which attestations they choose to promote or endorse, and they can stop their trademarks being used without permission in connection with an attestation. So while anyone a security attestation programme permits may issue an attestation, projects can influence which attestations their community and downstream users treat as authoritative.

An attestation issued without the project's involvement may also be worth less in practice. Whoever issues it may lack the maintainers' knowledge of the codebase, development practices and security processes, and any commitment from the maintainers to fix what the attestation finds.

Security attestations don't exist yet (see [[attestations/what-is-a-security-attestation]]). The CRA lets the Commission set up voluntary _security attestation programmes_ by delegated act, open to _"the developers or users"_ of open source software _"as well as other third parties"_ ([[CRA Article 25]]). The Commission's FAQ says only that such programmes _"can be used to assess the conformity of free and open-source components with the CRA"_ ([[official/faq_4-4-4]]); neither it nor the Commission's guidance says who may issue one. The CRA wants the programmes designed so that _"not only"_ those developing or contributing to the software _"can initiate or finance a security attestation but also third parties, such as manufacturers that integrate such products into their own products with digital elements, users, or Union and national public administrations"_ ([[CRA Recital 21]]). The Commission may clarify how the programmes work, and who may take part, as it creates them.
